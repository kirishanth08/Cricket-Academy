How Young Cricketers Build Better Habits Before Better Technique

For young players, the strongest foundation is not a complicated technique. It is a collection of simple habits that make learning easier, practice more productive and cricket more enjoyable.

12 Aug 2026• 8 min read• Academy Coaching Team

When a child starts cricket, it is tempting to focus immediately on the perfect cover drive, a faster bowling action or advanced tactical decisions. But young players learn differently. Before technique becomes reliable, they need habits that make good practice repeatable.

At the Under 10 stage especially, cricket should feel like a game first and a performance environment second. The aim is to help children become comfortable with the ball, their teammates, the space around them and the basic routines of training.

Start With Movement, Not Complexity

Good cricket begins with good movement. A young player who can balance, stop, change direction, throw and catch comfortably already has many of the physical ingredients needed to learn cricket skills.

That is why our early sessions include movement games alongside cricket drills. Children might sprint between markers, react to a coach's signal or compete in a catching challenge. These activities develop coordination without making fitness feel like a separate chore.

The first goal is not to make a young player look technically perfect. It is to make good movement feel natural.

Build A Simple Practice Routine

Young players benefit from repetition, but repetition works best when it has a clear structure. A simple routine might start with a short warm-up, move into one skill focus, introduce a game and finish with a quick review.

  • Arrive prepared and ready to participate.
  • Listen to one coaching point at a time.
  • Practise the same movement several times.
  • Apply the skill in a simple game.
  • Finish by identifying one thing to keep improving.

This routine gives children a predictable framework while leaving enough room for fun and exploration.

Confidence Comes From Small Wins

Confidence is not created by telling a child to be confident. It grows when they experience small, achievable successes. A clean catch, a well-timed shot, a successful run between wickets or a good bowling target can all become positive moments.

Coaches can help by setting challenges that are difficult enough to be meaningful but achievable enough to avoid frustration. The goal is steady progress rather than constant comparison with other players.

Let Mistakes Become Part Of Training

Young players will miss catches, swing and miss, bowl wide and make decisions that do not work. Those mistakes are useful. They show the player what to try differently next time.

A healthy training environment treats mistakes as information rather than failure. When children are comfortable making mistakes, they are more willing to attempt new skills and make decisions during games.

Technique Still Matters — At The Right Time

This does not mean technique should be ignored. Grip, stance, bowling action, catching position and throwing mechanics all matter. The difference is in how they are introduced.

For young players, technical coaching should be simple enough to remember and practical enough to use. Instead of giving a child five corrections at once, a coach can focus on one movement, let the player experience it and then add the next piece when they are ready.

Good coaching gives young players enough structure to improve and enough freedom to enjoy the game.

What Parents Can Do At Home

Parents do not need to become cricket coaches. In fact, keeping home practice simple can be more useful. Throw a soft ball for catching, play a reaction game, practise a short run between two markers or simply watch cricket together and ask what the player notices.

The most valuable thing parents can provide is encouragement and consistency. Ask what the child enjoyed, what they learned and what they want to try next. Those conversations help turn cricket into a long-term habit rather than a short-term activity.

The Bigger Goal

At the end of the Under 10 stage, success should not be measured only by runs scored or wickets taken. A young player who moves confidently, listens to coaching, works with teammates, understands basic game situations and wants to come back for the next session has already made meaningful progress.

Technical development becomes much easier when those foundations are in place. Build the habits first, keep the learning positive and allow the technique to develop with the player.
